CLINICAL PRESENTATION 17, 18
Vitiligo is clinically defined by the appearance of well-demarcated hypopigmented or
depigmented macules and patches, resulting from the selective loss of functional melanocytes.
These lesions are typically asymptomatic, though mild pruritus may occasionally be reported.
In individuals with darker skin tones, the contrast between affected and unaffected areas is
more pronounced. Lesion margins may exhibit intermediate hypopigmented zones, forming
characteristic patterns such as trichrome or hypochromic vitiligo. Poliosi- depigmentation of
hair within affected areas, often presenting as white locks on the scalp—is considered an
indicator of deeper melanocyte involvement. Due to the absence of melanin, depigmented
regions are more vulnerable to solar erythema. In cases of universal vitiligo, widespread
depigmentation is observed. Additionally, lesions may develop at sites of skin trauma, a
phenomenon known as koebnerization.
An international consensus in 2011 standardized the classification of vitiligo, distinguishing
segmental vitiligo (SV) from all other forms, which were grouped under non-segmental
vitiligo (NSV). The consensus also introduced mixed vitiligo—coexistence of SV and NSV
in a single patient—as a clinically distinct subgroup, categorized under NSV due to similar
pathophysiological behaviour.
