Vitiligo is a chronic, acquired autoimmune skin disorder characterized by well-demarcated
hypopigmented or depigmented macules and patches resulting from the selective destruction
of melanocytes in the epidermis. It may affect the skin, mucous membranes, and hair follicles,
and can be associated with systemic autoimmune conditions such as thyroiditis, uveitis, and
sensorineural hearing loss.
Before adressing vitiligo, it is important to understand the basic target cell that is affected
by the disease and for that a basic overview of the melanin pathway is essential. Melanin is
formed from the essential amino acids tyrosine and phenylalanine through a series of enzymatic
steps in the liver and skin. Tyrosine is formed in the liver by the hydroxylation of the essential
amino acid phenylalanine under the influence of phenylalanine hydroxylase which is in turn
converted to DOPA and further into the pigment under the influence of tyrosinase enzyme (Fig.
1). The melanin is made within the melanosomes which are of two types —eumelanosomes
(containing eumelanin), or pheomelanosomes (containing pheomelanin). These melanosomes
pass into the dendritic processes of the melanocyte to be further transferred into neighboring
keratinocytes.
